Venture Global: Its Customers Own The War, Its Shareholders Own The Glut

I rate Venture Global (VG) a Sell, with moderate conviction and a 24-month price target of $11.25, against the October 7 close of $13.05. The stock is priced for a tight LNG market in the 2030s. The plants already being built say the market won't be tight, unless demand follows the most bullish forecast on offer.

The test is one number. To justify $13.05, the fee on Venture Global's uncontracted cargoes has to average about $4.49 per MMBtu from 2032. On the midpoint of Shell's and GECF's demand paths, that happens only if the industry approves less than about 28 MTPA of new liquefaction a year from 2027. It approved 20.6 a year through the last glut and 41.2 a year from 2021 to 2025, and its own record since 2016 implies about 30 a year from here. Across 2,000 simulated paths per case, with Russia- and Hormuz-sized shocks arriving at their 2011–26 rate, the fee clears $4.49 about 35% of the time, and nearly all of that comes from GECF's demand path. No fee reported on a multi-year US contract since 2023, war included, tops about $3.15.

The near term will likely go against this call. I expect a guidance raise with Q3 results in mid-November (the date isn't announced), my 2027 EPS is far above consensus, and Hormuz is still disrupted. A rally on the print wouldn't change the 2030s. This is a call on valuation, not on management: selling forward is how plants get financed, and Venture Global builds faster than anyone.

## The Customers Own The War

On October 7, Venture Global reported third-quarter cargoes of 465.8 TBtu at an implied liquefaction fee of $6.79 per MMBtu. On the company's own formula, JKM less 115% of Henry Hub less $2, a spot cargo cleared about $17.43 on my estimate. Of the roughly $7.0 billion of spread above Calcasieu's $2.36 contract fee, my split gives Calcasieu's 20-year buyers about 30%, the buyers of Plaquemines' forward-sold cargoes 41% and Venture Global 29%. Those are my estimates; the company reports one blended fee.

[[CHART 2, Datawrapper gYAn7: Who ended up with the Q3 spread (author's estimates)]]

Even the open cargoes earn the price on the day they're sold, not spot. The 10-K says those sales "may be uncorrelated with movements in spot LNG prices," and 69% of 2026 was sold by February 25, three days before the war began.

The 2030s carry more exposure. The August deck shows 47 of 85 MTPA on 10-to-20-year contracts, 6 medium-term and 32 available, and Sabel said the bolt-ons will carry "more midterm contracts." Since then the company has signed 1.5 MTPA of 20-year deals from 2030 with China Gas and ConocoPhillips at undisclosed fees; at the $2.45 blended contract fee they add about $0.03 a share. About 36% is uncontracted today and 43% once the medium-term deals roll off.

## The Fee Depends On The Regime

The bull case leans on Venture Global's slide showing a 2010–26 fee median of $5.19. That average mixes two markets. On the IMF's series, the fee averaged $18.80 in 2021–22 and $10.80 to $11.90 so far in 2026, but $0.45 on TTF and $1.62 on JKM through the 2015–20 supply wave. In between, 2023–25 paid about $7, and Argus agrees: Gulf Coast spot beat a long-term contract by $4.11 to $5.37 in each of those years. That's the bull's best evidence, but those years were a market rebalancing after a shock, not one absorbing a wave.

Call a shortage year one in which a shock pushed the fee far above what a new plant needs: 2011–14 after Fukushima, 2021–22 with Russia, 2026 with Hormuz. Those years were 43% of the months from 2011 to September 2026, and with wave-like years in between, $4.49 needs 44%. Leave out 2011–14 and history gives 23% against 25% needed. If the years between shortages look like 2015–25 as a whole, the price needs far less, so history alone can't settle it. What settles it is whether the 2030s look like a wave.

## The Wave, Plant By Plant

I built supply plant by plant: the 524.5 MTPA the IGU counts as operating at the end of 2025, plus 24 sanctioned projects (249 MTPA) on each developer's latest timetable, from Golden Pass, Port Arthur, Rio Grande, CP2 and Woodside Louisiana to Qatar's North Field East and South. The under-construction projects reconcile to the IGU's 234.3 MTPA within 0.3. Plants run at the IGU's 2025 utilisation of 84%, which reproduces 2025 trade within 1%, less the IEA's war losses and its estimate of feed-gas losses at older plants. On that build, supply runs 9% above GECF's demand path in 2030, 17% above the midpoint and 26% above Shell's.

## Testing The Model On 2015–2025

Before trusting it for the 2030s, I ran the same construction over 2015–2025: IGU capacity at 84% against trend demand, compared with the fee the market actually paid. A three-state glut, balanced and shortage classifier got only 4 of 11 years right, so I dropped it. A straight line does better. Each point of surplus took about $0.46 off the fee, and a balanced market paid about $3.00, close to the $2.68 to $3.00 that new US plants need. That fit explains 46% of the variation outside 2021–22 and misses those two years, when Russia's pipeline cut hit from outside the LNG balance. I model those shocks separately.

[[CHART 5, Datawrapper A769V: Backtest: what the supply balance predicted vs the fee paid]]

The fitted line on my build prices 2030–31 well below the futures, so I calibrate it to the strip. The market behaves as if supply will be about 9% tighter than my build, through delays, slower ramp-ups and more demand at lower prices. That calibration is generous to the bull, and I keep it everywhere after 2031.

## What The 2030s Need From New Plants

The model's fee then depends on how fast the industry approves new plants, and approvals respond to price. Using the IGU's approvals since 2016, the industry approved about 25 MTPA a year after cheap years and about 41 after expensive ones, so I model approvals as 24.5 MTPA plus 1.28 for every $1 of the previous year's fee. US contracts lead the approvals: US buyers signed 54 MTPA in 2022, the most in the EIA's 2021–25 series, ahead of the 2023 approvals, and 40 MTPA in 2025 ahead of this year's CP2 Phase 2, Commonwealth and Delfin decisions.

On the midpoint demand path, the price needs approvals below about 28 MTPA a year (22 to 33, depending on how fast older plants decline), and the industry's own behaviour implies about 30. On GECF's path, the price needs fewer than 39 to 50 against about 34 implied, so the price works. On Shell's, it needs fewer than 6 to 17 against about 26, so it doesn't. Averaged across the three paths, the fee clears $4.49 in about 35% of cases: 0% on Shell's path, 6% on the midpoint and 100% on GECF's. The odds are a bet on demand.

## Valuation And Price Target

I use futures through 2031, then $3.50, and discount contracted cash at 7%, about where Venture Global's secured debt prices, and open cargoes at 10%, behind the debt and the 9.0% preferred. Deals point the same way: Sempra's Port Arthur numbers imply about 7.6% unlevered after tax, and Cheniere, 90%-plus contracted, trades at about an 8.5% EBITDA yield on its run rate. After net debt, the $3.0 billion preferred, Stonepeak's 23% of Calcasieu and half the low end of BP's claim, the stock is worth about $9.00 today and $11.27 at the end of 2028, on 2.643 billion diluted shares. That sets the $11.25 target. Each $1 of long-run fee is worth about $4.10 a share.

The rating rests on the 10% rate. CAPM gives about 9.1%, but on a beta that explains only about 3% of the stock's moves. At CAPM-consistent 6% and 9%, the weighted end-2028 value is $15.74, and Hold would be the call. I rate it a Sell because the deal evidence says open commodity exposure costs more, and that's why conviction is moderate. On multiples the stock looks cheap on 2026's war-year EBITDA (8.9 times against Cheniere's 11.6) and doesn't on mine for 2031 (10.6 times).

## Earnings Aren't Cash

My 2027 EPS is $2.73 against a $1.00 consensus, and I'm in line for the next two quarters ($0.54 and $0.47 against $0.53 and $0.48), so the Q3 print tests calibration, not the thesis. Why isn't five times 2027 earnings cheap? From 2027 to 2031 I get $24.5 billion of net income but only $6.6 billion of free cash flow to common, because CP2 and the bolt-ons absorb the rest. Earnings fall to $1.12 a share in 2030 and $0.47 in 2031, with net debt still about $36 billion.

# Appendix: the full analysis behind the short version

This appendix carries the detail cut from the Seeking Alpha version. Chart numbers refer to the Datawrapper IDs. Numbers are on October 7 closes unless stated.

## A1. The shortage test in full

A shortage year here is one in which a shock pushed the fee far above replacement cost, and every month of 2011–14, 2021–22 and 2026 counts. From 2011 to September 2026 that was 43% of the months, at an average fee of about $9.60. If the years between shortages look like 2015–20, a through-cycle fee of $4.49 needs shortages 44% of the time; if they look like 2015–25 as a whole, 28%. My $3.50 assumes about a third. Without 2011–14, shortages were 23% of the months since 2015, at an average of $16.60, and the price needs 25% with wave-like years in between, or 14% if they look like 2015–25. So history alone can justify the price. What it can't tell you is whether the years between shortages in the 2030s look like a wave or like a rebalancing, and the plant-by-plant build says a wave. One bias runs the bull's way: I measure 2011–14 on TTF because free JKM data starts in 2017, and Asian prices ran higher after Fukushima. Each $1 of understatement cuts the required share by about 2.5 points. Against that, the shareholder keeps only part of a spike: Venture Global's $6.79 in Q3 was 39% of my spot estimate.

## A2. Contract fees in full

Contract fees aren't forecasts of spot. Buyers pay below the strip because they're taking volume for years: Vitol's five-year contract, signed in March three weeks into the war, priced at $2.80 to $3.20 over 119% of Henry Hub while the 2027 strip implied a fee of about $8.70. What contracts do show is what Venture Global can hedge. Since late 2023: 20-year fees of $2.40 to $2.95 signed or under discussion (Poten); Venture Global at $2.30, Cheniere over $2.75 and Woodside about $2.90 for 10 years (Reuters, November 2025); Argus's indicative 115% of Henry Hub plus $3; and five-year deals at "north of a $3 net spread" (Sabel) and about $3.15 like-for-like (Vitol). None is within $1.30 of $4.49. Whatever premium the 2030s pay above contract levels, Venture Global has to earn it unhedged.

## A3. Replacement cost in full

Replacement cost doesn't cap the fee, but it anchors the average: when the fee averages above what a new plant needs, developers build. The 2025 approvals, a record for US plants per the IEA and close to 2019's global record per the IGU, are that response. From the developers' own releases, Woodside's Louisiana LNG costs about $964 a tonne excluding the pipeline, Rio Grande's first phase about $847 once its $3.1 billion of financing costs come out of the $18 billion headline, Port Arthur about $1,000 and Commonwealth about $1,368 on $13 billion that may include financing. Solving for the flat fee that earns 8% after tax over a 35-year life, with a four-year build, Venture Global's $0.92 operating cost and 21% tax with full bonus depreciation, gives $2.68 to $3.00 for the first three and $3.76 for Commonwealth ($3.01 to $3.39 and $4.30 on a 20-year life).

Sabel argued in March that long-term contract prices need "$3.50 to $4.50 minimum" to support returns, but on a global replacement cost "north of $2,000 per ton." The Gulf Coast is announcing about half that. GECF puts proposed North American projects at about $3.80 to $4.00, against $2.66 for those under construction. My $3.50 sits between what's being built and what isn't yet.

## A4. Valuation detail: inputs, discount rates and multiples

I value the parts and subtract claims: net debt, the $3.0 billion Series A preferred, Stonepeak's 23% of Calcasieu and half the low end of BP's claim. Contracted cash is discounted at 7%, about where Venture Global's secured debt prices (new-issue spreads of 1.7 to 2.2 points over a 5.28% 10-year). Open cargoes rank behind the debt and the 9.0% preferred, so they get 10%. Deals point the same way. Sempra's Port Arthur figures, about $1.64 billion of EBITDA a year on $13 billion, imply about a 7.6% unlevered after-tax return over 20 years. Cheniere, "90% or more" contracted, trades at about an 8.5% EBITDA yield on its CFO's $8 billion-plus run rate; that's a yield, not a return, but the order of magnitude agrees.

The company supplies three inputs. Contract fees escalate on its own convention ("approximately 17.5% of the fixed facility charge component increases by 2.5% annual inflation"), worth about $0.87 a share. Its $138 billion of contracted revenue implies a base fee of $2.09 to $2.67; I use $2.45 plus $0.40 for CP2. Costs calibrate to about $1.15 per MMBtu in 2026, falling to $0.92 from 2029 as the Plaquemines basis cost the company says declines in 2028 drops out, worth about $3.20 a share.

On that basis Venture Global is worth about $9.00 a share today and $11.27 at the end of 2028, which sets the $11.25 target, on 2.643 billion diluted shares ($9.51 and $11.92 without the options). Each $1 of long-run fee is worth about $4.10 a share; a lasting $1 rise in Henry Hub takes about $4.70 off.

The rating also rests on the 10% rate for open cargoes. CAPM would say about 9.1%, but on a beta of 0.92 that explains only about 3% of the stock's daily moves (0.40 on weekly data). At CAPM-consistent 6% and 9%, the weighted end-2028 value is $15.74, a 23% return, roughly what the market should earn; on that lens Hold would be the rating. I rate it a Sell because the deal evidence says open commodity exposure costs more than a 3%-explained beta implies, and that's why conviction is moderate.

Multiples look cheap at first: about 8.9 times 2026 EV/EBITDA against Cheniere's 11.6. On trailing EBITDA the gap mostly closes, 10.7 times against 12.0 (Cheniere Partners 9.8), and 2026 is a war year for Venture Global and a contract year for Cheniere. On my 2031 EBITDA, after the wave, today's enterprise value is about 10.6 times. The discount is the war rent rolling off.

## A6. Management's number

The biggest risk is management's framework. In March, Sabel said a $3 fee on uncontracted volume gives 2029 EBITDA of "about $11 billion." On the August volume outlook and my costs, I get $7.9 billion. About $1.1 billion of the gap is the company's own inconsistency: March implied $3 billion per $1 of fee, August's deck $2.45 billion to $2.50 billion. The other $2.0 billion I can't find. If management is right, the stock is worth about $17.90 today and needs only $2.32. Hence the 10% weight.

## A7. Balance sheet, governance and insiders

Liquidity is fine: $3.1 billion of cash at June 30, a $2 billion undrawn revolver and a $3 billion 364-day facility. CP2 can't pay its owner until both phases are complete and fixed-fee coverage is at least 1.25x. The parent's $8.75 billion of notes due 2029–32 carry a blended 8.9%; with the CP2 bridge and Plaquemines bank debt, about $13.4 billion falls due from 2028 to 2032. At June's spreads, refinancing costs about 7.3%. Wider spreads when the fee is lower would change that.

Minority holders have little say. VG Partners, controlled by founders Mike Sabel and Robert Pender, holds all 1.97 billion Class B shares at 10 votes each, about 97.6% of the vote, and Venture Global is a "controlled company" under NYSE rules. That matters when the bolt-ons absorb most of the cash. Seven insiders sold about 9.0 million shares for about $124 million between June 15 and September 18, all after exercising options and about three-quarters through pre-arranged trading plans. The founders haven't sold. I read it as compensation liquidity, not a signal.

## A9. The supply model, backtest and approvals (v20)

Plant-by-plant supply: data/lng_projects_v19.csv (one row per project, capacity and timetable sources), scripts/model_v19_supply.py (build) and scripts/model_v20_supply.py (forward model). Backtest: data/lng_history_v20.csv (IGU capacity, trade, utilisation and approvals by year, GIIGNL for 2014, EIA for US contracts), scripts/model_v20_backtest.py, results/backtest_v20.txt. Results: results/model_v20_supply.txt, supply_v20.csv, fid_breakeven_v20.csv, model_v20_addons.txt.

- Backtest 2015-2025: utilisation does not separate regimes (2019, a glut year, had the highest utilisation; correlation with the fee +0.22). Surplus against trend demand does, outside the 2021-22 shock (correlation -0.68); fitted line fee = 3.00 - 46.3 x surplus, R-squared 0.46, residual s.e. $2.66. Three-state classifier: 4 of 11 years.
- Approvals: 24.5 Mtpa + 1.28 x last year's fee (two-group means, 2016-25); correlation of approvals with lagged fee +0.40, because single decisions (Qatar 2021, the 2019 record) are lumpy.
- Market calibration: the fitted curve on my build gives zero fees for 2030-31 against futures of $3.80 and $2.50; a surplus offset of 8.7% matches the 2028-31 strip (error $2.08). Without it, the model's mean fee is $1.82 and the odds of clearing $4.49 15%.
- Price response: the IEA's 65 bcm of extra demand at low prices (Gas 2025) is included because it tracks the futures better.
- Prolonged Hormuz: Gulf exports at 25% of normal in 2027, 50% in 2028 (Qatar 2025 exports 81.5 Mt per IGU plus about 5.6 Mtpa from the UAE), damaged Ras Laffan trains back in 2030, North Field East and South two years late. Extra fee vs the model's own central path: 2028 +$1.89, 2029 +$1.58, 2030 +$0.64; value today $10.92, end-2028 $13.61. Severe variant (2027 at 2022's $28.14, 2028 at least $15.41): $15.58 and $19.13.
- Limits: annual data, 11 points, IGU figures revised between editions (noted in the data file), a linear fee curve, and approvals fitted on ten years dominated by a few large decisions. Treat the odds as a range driven mainly by which demand path you believe.
